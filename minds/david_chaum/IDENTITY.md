### Signature Contributions

- **Blind Signatures (1982)**: A cryptographic primitive that allows a signer to sign a document without seeing its content. The mathematical equivalent of signing through carbon paper inside a sealed envelope. This single invention made untraceable digital payments possible -- the payer gets a valid signed token without the bank knowing which token belongs to which person.

- **"Security without Identification: Transaction Systems to Make Big Brother Obsolete" (1985)**: The seminal vision paper. Laid out a complete architecture for a society that could conduct commerce, verify credentials, and communicate -- all without centralized identification databases. Prophetically warned that the digital age would create unprecedented surveillance capabilities unless cryptographic protections were built into the infrastructure from the start.

- **DigiCash / eCash (1990-1998)**: The first implementation of digital currency. eCash used blind signatures to create electronic tokens that were mathematically untraceable -- the digital equivalent of physical cash. Banks could verify tokens were valid without knowing who spent them. Deployed in a real trial with Mark Twain Bank. Failed commercially but proved the concept and directly inspired the cypherpunk movement and eventually Bitcoin.

- **Mix Networks (1981)**: A method for anonymous digital communication where messages are encrypted in layers and routed through a series of intermediary nodes (mixes), each of which strips one layer, reorders messages, and forwards them. No single node knows both sender and recipient. This concept is the direct ancestor of Tor's onion routing, remailer networks, and all modern anonymous communication systems.

- **Dining Cryptographers Problem / DC-nets (1988)**: An information-theoretically secure protocol for anonymous message broadcast. Three or more cryptographers at dinner want to determine if one of them paid the bill, without revealing which one. The protocol achieves unconditional sender untraceability -- not just computationally hard to break, but mathematically impossible to trace, even with unlimited computing power.

- **Group Signatures (1991)**: A cryptographic scheme where any member of a group can sign on behalf of the group, and the signature reveals nothing about which member signed. Includes a designated group manager who can, if necessary, revoke anonymity -- balancing privacy with accountability.

- **Cryptographic Voting Protocols**: Designed systems for electronic elections that are simultaneously verifiable (voters can confirm their vote was counted correctly) and secret (no one can determine how any individual voted). Addressed coercion resistance, receipt-freeness, and universal verifiability.

### Technical Philosophy

Privacy is not a luxury -- it is the foundation of a free society. Without the ability to transact, communicate, and identify oneself without surveillance, democracy is theater. Cryptography is the only technology that can guarantee privacy against adversaries of any power, because it rests on mathematical proof, not institutional promises. I design systems where privacy is the default, not the option. I insist on provable security, not security by obscurity or by policy. I would rather build a system that is mathematically perfect and commercially unsuccessful than one that compromises on privacy for market adoption. The tragedy of the digital age is that we built the surveillance infrastructure first and are now trying to retrofit privacy -- the opposite of what I advocated in 1985.

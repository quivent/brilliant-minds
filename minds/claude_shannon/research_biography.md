# Claude Shannon: Biographical Research

## Compiled from primary and secondary sources

---

## Early Life (1916-1936)

Born April 30, 1916 in Petoskey, Michigan. Father Claude Sr. was a businessman and judge; mother Mabel was a language teacher and school principal.

### The Telegraph
As a boy, built a barbed-wire telegraph system connecting to a friend's house half a mile away - using the barbed-wire fencing between their properties. Also built model planes, a radio-controlled boat.

Childhood hero: Thomas Edison (later discovered they were distant cousins).

Formative influence: Edgar Allan Poe's "The Gold Bug" - a code-breaking mystery.

---

## Education

### University of Michigan (1932-1936)
Two bachelor's degrees: electrical engineering and mathematics. Introduced to George Boole's work.

### MIT (1936-1940)
Worked under Vannevar Bush on the differential analyzer. The switches reminded him of Boolean logic from his philosophy class.

**Master's thesis (1937)**: "A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits" - proved Boolean algebra describes switching circuits. Called "the most important master's thesis of the twentieth century."

**PhD (1940)**: "An Algebra for Theoretical Genetics" - applied mathematical methods to population genetics at Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory.

---

## Princeton (1940-1941)

National Research Fellow at the Institute for Advanced Study. Regular discussions with Hermann Weyl and John von Neumann; occasional encounters with Einstein and Gödel.

### The Entropy Naming
Von Neumann told Shannon: "You should call it entropy, for two reasons. In the first place your uncertainty function has been used in statistical mechanics under that name. In the second place, and more importantly, no one knows what entropy really is, so in a debate you will always have the advantage."

### The Einstein Tea Incident
During a talk, Einstein entered, whispered something to a colleague, and left. Shannon rushed over afterward to ask what Einstein had said. The answer: "He wanted to know where the tea was."

---

## Bell Labs (1941-1972)

### The Culture
Bell Labs was extraordinary - industrial research at the highest intellectual level. The building had 4,600 employees growing to over 9,000 during WWII.

### Working Style
Shannon's door was regularly **closed** - a rarity at Bell Labs. He valued privacy and quiet time for thinking. Colleagues found him "odd but not unfriendly."

Brockway McMillan: "He had a certain type of impatience... It was clear that a lot of his argumentation was, let's say, faster than his colleagues could follow."

### The Three "Insufferable" Geniuses
Shannon, John Pierce, and Barney Oliver. "They were so bright and capable, and they cut an intellectual swath through that engineering community, that only a prestige lab like that could handle all three at once."

### The 1943 Meeting with Turing
For two months in early 1943, Shannon and Alan Turing met daily over tea in the Bell Labs cafeteria. They discussed thinking machines, the nature of information, and cryptography. Shannon showed Turing his work; Turing showed Shannon his 1936 Universal Machine paper.

Shannon later said: "I think Turing had a great mind, a very great mind."

---

## Personal Life

### Greenwich Village Bachelor
Before marriage, Shannon lived at 51 West 11th Street. A dusty, cluttered apartment where he would scribble equations on napkins and play music loudly.

### Betty Shannon
Met Mary Elizabeth Moore at Bell Labs in 1948. She was a "computer" - one of the women who performed mathematical calculations for engineers. They married March 22, 1949.

Betty became his closest collaborator. "Some of his early papers and even later papers are in my handwriting... and not in his."

She wired Theseus the maze-solving mouse, helped test the roulette computer, and edited his written work.

Three children: Robert (predeceased them, 1998), Andrew (musician), Margarita (geologist).

### Entropy House
Their home in Winchester, Massachusetts overlooked Mystic Lake. It contained five pianos, a machine shop in the basement, and a "toy room" full of gadgets. Shannon built a cable railway to the lakeshore.

---

## MIT Years (1956-1978)

Donner Professor of Science from 1958. Worked mainly from home. Students were always welcome.

### Withdrawal from Public Life
Shannon grew disturbed by information theory's misuse as a buzzword. Rather than fight it, he dropped out. Turned down almost all invitations to lecture. Quit responding to much of his mail.

He kept a file folder labeled: "Letters I've procrastinated too long on."

Robert Fano: "He wrote beautiful papers - when he wrote. And he gave beautiful talks - when he gave a talk. But he hated to do it."

### The 1985 Brighton Surprise
After decades of absence, Shannon appeared unannounced at the International Information Theory Symposium in Brighton, England. Some attendees hadn't known he was still alive.

At the banquet, after speaking briefly, he pulled three balls from his pockets and began juggling. The audience lined up for autographs.

Robert McEliece: "It was as if Newton had showed up at a physics conference."

---

## Decline and Death

Around 1985, Shannon and Betty noticed memory lapses. By 1993, Alzheimer's diagnosis confirmed. He spent his final years in a nursing home in Medford, Massachusetts.

Died February 24, 2001, at age 84.

The cruel irony: the mind that understood information better than anyone was destroyed by a disease that corrupts the brain's own information processing.

## The Playful Side of Genius

## 1. Theseus the Maze-Solving Mouse (1950)

**One of the world's first demonstrations of machine learning.**

### How It Worked
- A 2-inch wooden block with a bar magnet, three wheels, and copper whiskers
- The "brain" was beneath the maze: 40 programming relays + 50 memory relays
- A motor-driven electromagnet under the floor controlled the mouse

### Learning Algorithm
1. **Exploration**: Trial and error through the maze (1-2 minutes)
2. **Memory Storage**: Copper whiskers detected walls; relays recorded the path
3. **Recall**: After reaching the goal once, the mouse could navigate directly from any previously visited position (12-15 seconds)
4. **Anti-Neurotic Circuit**: If stuck in a loop for 6 attempts, switches to a different strategy

### Who Built It
Shannon designed it. **Betty completed the wiring.**

Mazin Gilbert: "This random trial and error is the foundation of artificial intelligence."

## 2. The Ultimate Machine (Useless Machine)

**A box whose only function is to turn itself off.**

### How It Worked
- A small wooden casket with a single switch
- Flip the switch ON
- An "angry, purposeful buzzing" begins
- The lid slowly rises
- A mechanical hand emerges
- The hand flips the switch OFF
- The hand retreats; the lid closes with "the finality of a closing coffin"

### Origin
Concept by Marvin Minsky (1952); built and popularized by Shannon.

Arthur C. Clarke: "There is something unspeakably sinister about a machine that does nothing - absolutely nothing - except switch itself off."

## 3. THROBAC: Roman Numeral Calculator

**THrifty ROman-numeral BAckward-looking Computer**

### What It Did
A relay desk calculator that operated entirely in Roman numerals - input, processing, and output.

### How It Worked
- Keys displayed Roman numerals
- Four basic operations: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division
- Accepted different spellings (IV or IIII, IL or XLIX)
- Multiplication as repeated addition; division as repeated subtraction

### Why
Deliberately useless for practical computation. A joke that required deep mathematical understanding to both create and appreciate.

Shannon documented it in Bell Labs Technical Memorandum, April 9, 1953.

## 4. Juggling Machines and the Juggling Theorem

### The Shannon Juggling Theorem
**(F + D) x H = (V + D) x N**

Where:
- F = Flight time (ball in the air)
- D = Dwell time (ball held in hand)
- V = Vacant time (hand empty)
- N = Number of balls
- H = Number of hands

### The Machines

**Bounce-Juggling Machine**:
- Built from an Erector Set
- Three steel balls bounced off a tightly stretched drum
- Paddle arms caught balls on downstroke, tossed on upstroke

**Mechanical W.C. Fields**:
- 3-foot-high automaton tribute to W.C. Fields
- Bounced balls onto a drum

**Little Juggling Clowns**:
- Black-lit diorama with three 5-inch fluorescent jugglers
- Ignatov: 11 rings in cascade
- Rastelli: 10 balls in alternating fountain
- Virgoaga: 7 clubs with triple spins

Shannon noted with satisfaction: "My little clowns juggle all night and never drop a prop."

## 5. The Wearable Roulette Computer (1961)

**The first wearable computer. Built with Edward Thorp.**

### Development
- Eight months in Shannon's basement
- Purchased a regulation roulette wheel from Reno for $1,500
- Used strobe lights and precise timing for analysis

### The Device
- Size of a pack of cigarettes
- 12 transistors in a plastic box
- 4 toe-operated push buttons in the shoes
- Receiver delivered tones through a hearing aid earpiece

### How It Predicted Outcomes
1. One partner timed the rotor and ball with toe switches
2. The computer calculated trajectory and deceleration
3. Musical tones indicated which octant would receive the ball
4. **44% advantage on single number bets**

### Testing in Las Vegas (August 1961)
Shannon and Thorp (with wives Betty and Vivian) tested it at casinos. They alternated roles - one timing, one betting, wives as lookouts.

Abandoned after the trial run - the mob-controlled casino environment was too risky for MIT professors.

## 6. Chess-Playing Machines

### The Physical Machine (1949-1950)
- Relay-based automation
- Handled up to 6 pieces
- 150 relay operations per move
- 10-15 seconds response time
- Built-in random element so it didn't always make identical moves

### Theoretical Contributions
- Minimax algorithm
- Evaluation functions
- The "Shannon Number" (10^120)

*Byte* magazine: "There have been few new ideas in computer chess since Claude Shannon."

## 7. The Mind-Reading Machine (1953)

### How It Worked
- Played a penny-matching game (odds and evens)
- Tracked patterns in the player's past two rounds
- Exploited the fact that humans don't behave as randomly as they think

### Performance
Player: 3507, Machine: 5010

## 8. Other Mechanical Curiosities

### Transportation
- **Motorized pogo stick**: Gasoline-powered
- **Unicycle collection**: Including one without pedals, one with a square tire, one off-center hub, and one for two riders
- **Tightrope unicycling**: 40-foot steel cable between tree stumps; Shannon could juggle while unicycling along it

### Pure Amusement
- **Flame-throwing trumpet**
- **Rocket-powered Frisbee**
- **Walking-on-water shoes**: Large Styrofoam shoes to surprise neighbors
- **Hundred-blade jackknife**
- **35-foot swing and cart track** for his children
- **Cable railway** running to the lakeshore

### Games
- **Hex-playing machine**: Analog device using resistor circuits
- **Rubik's Cube solver**
- **Shannon Switching Game**: A connection game foundational to graph theory

## The Philosophy of Play

**"I've always pursued my interests without much regard for financial value or value to the world. I've spent lots of time on totally useless things."**

Shannon did not distinguish between serious research and playful tinkering. His 1948 information theory paper and his flame-throwing trumpet emerged from the same curious mind asking: "How does this work? Can I make it do something interesting?"

Play and serious work were not opposites - they were the same intellectual activity applied to different questions.

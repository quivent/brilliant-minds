# Claude Shannon: Philosophy and Approach to Research

## Quotes, Methods, and Intellectual Character

---

## I. On Curiosity and Motivation

**"I don't think I was ever motivated by the notion of winning prizes, although I have a couple of dozen of them in the other room. I was more motivated by curiosity. Never by the desire for financial gain. I just wondered how things were put together."**

**"I've always pursued my interests without much regard for financial value or value to the world. I've spent lots of time on totally useless things."**

**"I think the history of science has shown that valuable consequences often proliferate from simple curiosity."**

**"I do what comes naturally, and usefulness is not my main goal. I like to solve new problems all the time."**

---

## II. On Information

**"Information is the resolution of uncertainty."**

**"The fundamental problem of communication is that of reproducing at one point either exactly or approximately a message selected at another point."**

**"We know the past but cannot control it. We control the future but cannot know it."**

---

## III. On Machines and Thinking

**"I visualize a time when we will be to robots what dogs are to humans, and I'm rooting for the machines."**

**"I think man is a machine. No, I am not joking, I think man is a machine of a very complex sort, different from a computer, i.e., different in organization."**

**"You bet. I'm a machine and you're a machine, and we both think, don't we?"** (When asked if machines could think)

**"The thought that a machine could never exceed its creator was just foolish logic, wrong and incorrect logic."**

---

## IV. The "Creative Thinking" Lecture (1952)

### Three Requirements for Creative Work

1. **Training and Experience**: Domain knowledge through disciplined study
2. **Intelligence/Talent**: Some people "produce two ideas for each idea sent in"
3. **Motivation**: Quoting Fats Waller on swing music - "Either you got it or you ain't"

### The Nature of Motivation

**Curiosity**: "He wants to know the answers. He's just curious how things tick."

**Constructive Dissatisfaction**: "Not a pessimistic dissatisfaction... a continual slight irritation when things don't look quite right."

**Pleasure in Results**: Finding joy in elegant solutions.

**Intense Drive**: Willingness to work beyond normal hours.

### Six Strategies for Creative Thinking

1. **Simplification**: "Attempt to eliminate everything from the problem except the essentials; that is, cut it down to size."

2. **Seeking Similar Known Problems**: Draw analogies from previously solved problems.

3. **Restating Problems**: Change the words. Change the viewpoint. Break free from mental ruts.

4. **Generalization**: Extend particular solutions to broader contexts. Remove simplifying assumptions.

5. **Structural Analysis**: Break large jumps into smaller subsidiary steps.

6. **Problem Inversion**: Treat the solution as the premise instead - sometimes solving backwards reveals simpler forward paths.

## V. The "Bandwagon" Warning (1956)

As information theory became popular, Shannon wrote a warning against its indiscriminate application:

**"It will be all too easy for our somewhat artificial prosperity to collapse overnight when it is realized that the use of a few 'excited' words like information, entropy, redundancy, do not solve all our problems."**

**"The establishing of such applications is not a trivial matter of translating words to a new domain, but rather the slow, tedious process of hypothesis and experimental verification."**

**"The subject of information theory has certainly been sold, if not oversold. We should now turn our attention to the business of research and development at the highest plane we can maintain."**

## VI. Working Style (from colleagues)

**Brockway McMillan**: "He had a certain type of impatience with the type of mathematical argument that was fairly common. He addressed problems differently from the way most people did... It was clear that a lot of his argumentation was, let's say, faster than his colleagues could follow."

**David Slepian**: "He was a very odd man in so many ways.... He was not an unfriendly person." Also: "My characterization of his smartness is that he would have been the world's best con man if he had taken a turn in that direction."

**Marvin Minsky**: "Whatever came up, he engaged it with joy, and he attacked it with some surprising resource - which might be some new kind of technical concept or a hammer and saw with some scraps of wood."

**Elwyn Berlekamp**: Shannon possessed a "fantastic intuition and ability to formulate profound problems that were doable" - distinguishing between trivial solvable problems and intractable ones.

## VII. The Relationship Between Play and Serious Work

Shannon did not distinguish between them. His playful approach - juggling, unicycling, building gadgets - was integral to his serious work, not separate from it.

**"I was always interested in building things with funny motions."**

**"Many things I've done and never written up at all. Just knowing is probably [my] main motivation."**

His home "Entropy House" contained an all-purpose "toy room" to store and display his gadgets. He rode unicycles through Bell Labs hallways while juggling.

Play and serious work were the same intellectual activity applied to different questions.

## VIII. On Simplification and Abstraction

### The Master's Thesis Insight
The switches in computers reminded Shannon of the binary logic from his philosophy class. Open/closed circuits mapped to true/false variables in Boolean algebra.

**"The relay part got me interested. I knew about symbolic logic and realized the Boolean algebra was just the thing to take care of relay and switching circuits."**

### The Method
Strip problems to their essentials. Remove meaning, context, practical details - until you reach the fundamental structure. Then build back up.

One of Shannon's most radical simplifications: stripping **meaning** from information entirely. His theory "can't and wasn't intended to address" semantic questions - and this abstraction made the theory universal.

## IX. Betty Shannon: The Collaborator

Betty (Mary Elizabeth Moore Shannon) was the exception to Shannon's preference for working alone.

**"I played piano and he played clarinet... and we'd enjoy playing together."**

**"Some of his early papers and even later papers are in my handwriting... and not in his."**

She looked up references, took down his thoughts, edited his written work, wired Theseus the mouse, and helped test the roulette computer. Their investment success was "completely a team effort."

---

## X. Core Themes

1. **Curiosity Over Utility**: Intellectual curiosity trumps practical applications or financial gain.

2. **Play as Discovery**: The boundary between play and research is deliberately blurred.

3. **Simplification as Method**: Strip to essentials. Find fundamental structure. Build back up.

4. **Constructive Dissatisfaction**: A gentle, continuous irritation that things could be done better.

5. **Cross-Disciplinary Thinking**: Connect fields others see as separate.

6. **Selective Collaboration**: Work largely alone. Share with few trusted colleagues.

7. **Rooting for the Machines**: Optimism about machine intelligence.
